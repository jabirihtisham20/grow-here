Digital minimalism is not about rejecting technology. It is about choosing which digital tools deserve access to your time and attention. A useful app can stay. A social network can stay. The difference is that you decide when and why you use them instead of allowing every notification and recommendation to decide for you.

<Tip title="App Friction Technique">
Remove social media and news apps from your primary home screen. Requiring a browser login or searching via app library adds just enough friction to curb impulse scrolling.
</Tip>

## Start with your values, not your screen-time number

Ask what technology is supposed to support: work, learning, relationships, creativity, navigation or entertainment. A three-hour screen session can be valuable if it is deliberate work, while ten minutes of compulsive checking can feel draining. Focus on quality as well as quantity.

## Audit your attention leaks

For three days, notice what triggers unplanned phone use. Common triggers include boredom, notifications, waiting, difficult tasks and opening one app out of habit. Write down the top three. Those are the defaults to redesign first.

## Turn off non-essential notifications

Keep alerts that are genuinely time-sensitive, such as calls from important contacts, security warnings or necessary work messages. Disable promotional alerts, social recommendations, game reminders and most app badges.

## Simplify the home screen

Keep only essential daily tools on the first screen. Move entertainment and social apps into folders or the app library. Adding a small amount of friction makes automatic checking less convenient.

## Create intentional check-in times

Instead of checking email or social feeds constantly, choose a few windows during the day. This is especially useful for apps that matter but rarely require immediate responses.

## Replace, do not only remove

If you cut an hour of scrolling but have nothing else to do, the habit will return. Prepare attractive alternatives: a book, a walk, a phone call, cooking, exercise, a hobby or simply a quiet break.

## Use full-screen work blocks

When doing focused work, close unnecessary tabs, silence alerts and keep the phone out of reach. One task on one screen is often more effective than adding another productivity app.

## Clean digital clutter

Delete unused apps, remove duplicate screenshots, archive old downloads and unsubscribe from low-value email. The goal is not an empty device; it is a device where useful information is easier to find.

## Decide what 'enough' looks like

Set a small number of personal rules you can remember. For example: no social apps before breakfast, phone charges outside the bedroom, and messages checked at lunch and late afternoon. Simple rules are easier to maintain.

## Review after two weeks

Notice whether you feel more focused, less rushed or more present. Keep what works and change what feels unnecessarily restrictive. Digital minimalism should increase freedom, not become another perfection project.
