The 80/20 decluttering method is a practical idea: aim to use roughly 80% of a storage area and leave about 20% open. It is not a mathematical rule you need to measure with a ruler. It is a visual reminder that storage works better when it has breathing room.

<Tip title="Focus on High-Impact 20%">
Identify the 20% of belongings you use 80% of the time, and keep those accessible. The remaining items can be systematically evaluated or relocated to deeper storage.
</Tip>

## Why empty space is useful

When a drawer or shelf is packed completely, putting one item away often means moving several others. That friction encourages clutter to collect on nearby surfaces. A little open capacity makes daily tidying faster and gives temporary or new items somewhere to go.

## Start with one contained area

Choose a single drawer, shelf or cabinet. Remove everything, wipe the space and sort the contents into keep, relocate, donate or recycle/discard. Put back the items that genuinely belong there, starting with the things you use most.

## Stop before the space is full

As you return items, leave visible room around them. The exact amount will vary. A pantry shelf may work well at 80% capacity, while a drawer of socks might be fine with less empty space. The principle is usability, not perfection.

## Use boundaries instead of endless containers

Bins and dividers are helpful when they define categories, but too many can reduce usable space. Give each category a clear boundary and allow the boundary to limit how much you keep. When the container is comfortably full, review before adding more.

## Apply the method to wardrobes

An overfilled wardrobe makes it hard to see clothing and encourages forgotten purchases. Leave enough space for hangers to move. Keep only clothes that fit, suit your life and are in usable condition. Store off-season items separately if that makes daily access easier.

## Try it in the kitchen

Keep high-use cooking tools accessible and reduce duplicates. Leave some shelf capacity for groceries and leftovers rather than filling every cabinet with long-term stock. In the fridge, open space also makes it easier to see what needs using.

## Use it for digital clutter too

The same idea can guide digital spaces. You do not need to fill every gigabyte simply because storage is available. Archive old downloads, remove duplicate screenshots and keep your desktop or home screen intentionally simple.

## Do not turn decluttering into punishment

The goal is not to own the minimum possible. Sentimental, creative and hobby items can stay if they matter to you and have a reasonable home. The method should make the space easier to use, not make you anxious about keeping things.

## Review before buying more storage

If a space repeatedly fills beyond capacity, ask why. Is the category growing? Are items stored in the wrong place? Are you buying duplicates? A new cabinet may be necessary, but first check whether the existing system reflects your current life.

## Maintain with a one-in review

When something new enters a full category, quickly review what is already there. You do not need a strict one-in-one-out rule, but adding a new item is a useful trigger to remove something that no longer earns its space.
