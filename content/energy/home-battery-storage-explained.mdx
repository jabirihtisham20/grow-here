Home batteries store electricity so it can be used later. They are often paired with solar, but some households use batteries without solar to shift electricity from cheaper tariff periods to more expensive ones. Whether that saves money depends heavily on local prices, rules and how the system is configured.

<Tip title="Time-of-Use Arbitrage">
A home battery can be charged during cheap off-peak night hours and discharged during peak evening pricing, delivering significant savings even without rooftop solar.
</Tip>

## What a home battery does

A battery charges from solar, the grid or both, depending on system design. Later it discharges to supply household loads. An inverter manages the conversion between battery power and the electricity used by the home.

## Solar self-consumption

Without a battery, excess daytime solar may be exported. A battery can store part of that surplus for evening use. This can increase the share of solar consumed on site, which may improve economics where exported electricity is paid less than imported electricity costs.

## Time-of-use shifting

Some tariffs charge less at night and more during peak periods. A battery can charge when electricity is cheaper and discharge later. The value depends on the price difference, battery efficiency, cycle limits and any rules on grid charging.

## Backup power

Backup capability is not automatic. Some batteries can supply selected circuits or the whole home during an outage, while others cannot operate when the grid is down. Ask exactly what will continue working and for how long.

## Battery capacity versus power

Capacity, measured in kilowatt-hours, tells you roughly how much energy the battery stores. Power, measured in kilowatts, tells you how much it can deliver at once. A battery can have enough energy for several hours but still be unable to run multiple high-power appliances simultaneously.

## Do not size from guesswork

Use actual household data if available. A good installer should look at daily consumption, peak loads, solar generation and outage priorities. Bigger is not automatically better because unused capacity still costs money.

## Understand round-trip efficiency

Some energy is lost while charging and discharging. Round-trip efficiency describes how much stored energy you get back. Use realistic efficiency assumptions when estimating savings.

## Warranties and cycle life

Battery warranties often include years, energy throughput, cycles or a retained-capacity guarantee. Read the details and understand what usage patterns are covered.

## Safety and installation

Home batteries are electrical equipment that may require specific fire, ventilation, clearance and grid-interconnection rules. Use qualified installers and approved equipment. Do not treat battery installation as a DIY project unless local regulations explicitly allow it and you are suitably qualified.

## Is a battery worth it without solar?

It can be, particularly where peak electricity is much more expensive than off-peak electricity or where backup has high personal value. In a flat-tariff market with reliable supply, the financial case may be weaker.

## Ask for a simple savings model

Before buying, request a year-by-year estimate showing tariff assumptions, battery degradation, efficiency, maintenance and any export impact. If the proposal depends on aggressive future electricity-price increases, ask for a conservative scenario too.
